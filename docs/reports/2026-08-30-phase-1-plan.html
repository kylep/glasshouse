Phase 1 — Build Plan / 2026-08-30 / Xcode 26.2 · Swift 6.2.3 · iOS 26.2 sim / shipping iOS 26.6.1

Glasshouse

An iPhone app that reads every sensor it can legally reach, and shows you the readings — so the abstract claim “your phone knows everything” becomes a list of numbers you can scroll. Phase 1 builds the app and the agent harness that writes it. Phase 2, later, decides whether any of it is allowed to leave the device.

The two costs

Cost 1 — the simulator hides every entitlement error

I verified this locally: iPhoneSimulator.platform/Info.plist sets CODE_SIGNING_ALLOWED = NO. Simulator builds are not signed, carry no provisioning profile, and have no entitlements to enforce. Every restricted framework — SensorKit, FamilyControls, CoreNFC, NetworkExtension — is present in the simulator SDK and links cleanly.

So the app will build and run perfectly while containing capabilities your free account can never sign. The failure moves from build time to first device deploy, all at once. The harness has to carry that constraint as data, because the compiler won't. That is what the Capability Ledger in §3 exists for.

Cost 2 — a free team is more capable than expected, and capped where it hurts

The pleasant surprise: HealthKit works on a free personal team, as do App Groups, Keychain Sharing, HomeKit, and — because UIBackgroundModes is an Info.plist array rather than a portal capability — all background modes, including background location and bluetooth-central. That is a genuinely invasive app on a $0 account.

The wall: no Push, no iCloud, no NFC, no Network Extensions, no App Attest, no Wi-Fi SSID, no Sign in with Apple. And the two hungriest sensor entitlements — SensorKit and Family Controls — need a paid account plus Apple approval, with SensorKit gated on an approved research study. Those are out of reach, permanently, for a personal project.

Operationally: profiles expire in 7 days, 10 concurrent App IDs, 3 devices per platform. Plan for re-signing as routine, not incident.

02Reality

What “invasive af” actually buys you

I ran several research agents against Apple's live documentation, developer forums, and DTS answers rather than trusting training data, then verified the simulator surface against your local Xcode. Some of what came back corrected my own assumptions. The honest capability map:

Tier 1 — free, sandboxed, genuinely rich

This is the app's real content. None of it needs a dollar or an entitlement request:

  • Core Motion — accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, fused device motion, pedometer, barometric altimeter, motion activity classification, and AirPods head orientation via CMHeadphoneMotionManager.
  • Core Location — coordinates, altitude, speed, visits, significant-change monitoring, region monitoring, and the precise-vs-reduced accuracy distinction, which is itself a great thing to show the user. Use the modern async surface (CLLocationUpdate.liveUpdates(), CLServiceSession, CLMonitor) rather than the delegate API — it's the current recommendation and it's clean under Swift 6 concurrency. Heading and compass are the exception: they need the magnetometer, so they're device-only.
  • HealthKit — free-team eligible, the deepest well on the device, and it works in the simulator. That combination makes it the highest-value thing you can build against right now without hardware.
  • Vision — faces, OCR, barcodes, body pose, saliency. Needs no permission at all, because it's pure computation over images you already have. In the simulator it's partial: text recognition works, but every neural-engine-backed request (faces, barcodes, saliency, body pose) fails with Could not create inference context. Plan OCR for the simulator and everything else for a device.
  • Photos — per-asset EXIF and embedded GPS. Showing someone a map of where their camera roll was taken is the single most visceral demo in this app.
  • Contacts, Calendar, Reminders, camera, microphone, speech — the classic TCC set.
  • Core Bluetooth — no entitlement needed, and a promiscuous BLE scan reveals the surrounding device population. Highest-value sensor that is completely unavailable in the simulator.
  • Unprompted device state — battery, thermal state, low-power mode, disk, uptime, locale, accessibility settings, network path, and pasteboard metadata. No permission dialog at all, which is precisely the point worth making to the user.
One sensor the hardware has and no app can ever read

The ambient light sensor has no public API. The only surface for it anywhere in the SDK is SRSensorAmbientLightSensor, behind SensorKit's research gate. Same story for the proximity sensor's raw values. Worth featuring rather than omitting — “here is a sensor in your phone that no third-party app is allowed to read” is exactly the kind of thing this app exists to say.

The pasteboard nuance is a feature, not a footnote

Reading .string raises the “pasted from” banner. But hasStrings, hasURLs, types, numberOfItems, and the whole detectPatterns(for:) family read the clipboard's shape with no banner and no prompt. An app can know you have a URL on your clipboard, silently. Surface that in the UI with the banner-vs-silent distinction made explicit — it teaches the exact lesson the app exists to teach.

Tier 3 — impossible, and the sidequest lives here

No sandboxed app can read another app's permissions. At any price.

Apple DTS, March 2026, answering this exact question: Apple does not provide the APIs. There is no cross-app TCC read — there isn't even a unified API for your own app's permission state. Enumerating installed apps is equally closed: LSApplicationWorkspace needs private entitlements no third-party profile can carry, and canOpenURL is presence-only, capped, and deprecated in iOS 27.

One trap worth naming: LSApplicationWorkspace.allApplications returns a populated array in the simulator (no real installd to refuse it) and a silent empty array on device. A simulator-only workflow will happily “prove” that a dead API works. Do not build on it; it is also an automatic App Review rejection by static scan.

So the sidequest survives, but only through one door, and it is a good door:

The App Privacy Report import

iOS records, per app, every access to camera, microphone, photos, contacts, location, media library, and screen recording — plus every network domain contacted — on a rolling 7-day window. The user can export it as NDJSON from the share button in Settings. Your app receives that file through a share extension or document picker and parses it locally.

Zero entitlements. Works sideloaded. Real bundle IDs, real timestamps, real domains. Two App Store apps already ship this exact feature, one updated as recently as July 2026, which is good evidence the format is still current.

Three consequences to design around now: the 7-day window means you must import repeatedly and accumulate history locally, and turning the report off wipes it — so onboarding has to walk the user through switching it on and warn them. Records carry bare bundle IDs, so you need a name/icon resolver; itunes.apple.com/lookup?bundleId=… does that (~20 req/min, cache hard) but returns no privacy labels. And the domainOwner field is usually empty, which a bundled copy of DuckDuckGo's Tracker Radar fixes offline.

Unresolved — and a real Phase 1 task, not a footnote

My research agents returned two different schemas for this file. One reports a current v4 shape keyed on type/category with intervalBegin/intervalEnd pairs joined on a UUID; the other cites an older v3 shape keyed on stream/tccService. The v3 form appears to be an iOS 15 beta format preserved in a community parser repo, but I could not confirm that from Apple — Apple documents the report's contents and never documents the export format at all.

The parser therefore gets built schema-first against a real export from your own phone, with a versioned decoder and a deliberate hard failure on unrecognised shapes. This is Task 0 of that workstream, and it needs your device even though the rest of Phase 1 doesn't.

The most interesting product idea in the whole project falls out of this: put declared behaviour next to observed behaviour. The App Store privacy label is what an app claims it collects; the NDJSON is what it did. The gap between them is the story. Note that labels have no API — only scraping apps.apple.com — so scope that as a stretch goal behind a cache, and get comfortable with the terms-of-service question before it ships anywhere.

03Architecture

A shape that agents can actually work in

Two constraints drive the whole layout: Xcode project files are hostile to automated editing, and xcodebuild test against a simulator is far too slow to sit inside a red-green-refactor loop. Both are solvable, and solving them is most of what makes the harness work.

Logic lives in a Swift package, not the app

Everything with behaviour goes into an SPM library that builds and tests on macOS. swift test then runs in seconds with no simulator, no signing, and no Xcode project involved — which is the loop agents can iterate in. The iOS app target stays a thin shell.

Core Motion doesn't exist on macOS, so the split is drawn deliberately: the protocol layer and every piece of logic are platform-free and fully testable; the live sensor adapters are #if canImport(...)-guarded, iOS-only, and kept as thin as they can possibly be. Anything worth testing lives on the macOS side of that line.

The Xcode project is generated, never edited

A declarative project.yml (XcodeGen) generates the .xcodeproj, which is gitignored entirely. No agent ever touches a .pbxproj, no merge conflicts in generated XML, and — critically — the Info.plist usage strings are generated from the Capability Ledger rather than hand-maintained.

Every sensor is one protocol with three implementations

A sensor is an identifier, its ledger metadata, a start/stop, and a stream of samples. Each gets:

  • Live — the real framework call. iOS only. Deliberately trivial.
  • Fake — deterministic, seeded, no I/O. What the tests run against.
  • Replay — plays back a recorded fixture. What the simulator UI runs against.

This is what makes simulator-only development viable rather than merely tolerable: the entire UI can be built and demoed against realistic accelerometer and GPS traces while the device sits in a drawer. It also means the day you finally get hardware, the work is writing thin adapters against an interface that's already proven, not discovering your architecture.

The Capability Ledger

One YAML file is the spine of the project. Each sensor declares its framework, exact Info.plist key, required entitlement, whether a free team can sign it, its true simulator behaviour, its data-sensitivity class, and a source URL for each claim.

- id: core_motion.altimeter_absolute
  framework: CoreMotion
  plist_key: NSMotionUsageDescription
  entitlement: null
  free_team: true
  simulator: returns_nothing     # no motion injection exists
  sensitivity: high              # absolute altitude ≈ location
  status: not_started
  verified: 2026-08-30
  source: https://developer.apple.com/...

It earns its place by being executable. Tests assert that every registered sensor has a ledger row, that every row with a plist_key produces that key in the generated Info.plist, that nothing marked free_team: false is reachable from the shipping target, and that no row's verified date has gone stale. The ledger also generates the docs table and, ultimately, the app's own explanatory copy — the ledger is the product's content, not scaffolding around it.

04Harness

The agent fleet

Six project agents in .claude/agents/. The split is deliberate: expensive models where judgement is needed, cheap ones where the work is mechanical, and one agent whose only job is to be sceptical about Apple.

apple-api-researcher

opus · web + read

Verifies every Apple claim against live docs before anyone writes code, and fills in ledger rows with sources. Runs first on each sensor.

sensor-implementer

opus · full tools

Takes one ledger row and TDDs it: fake first, tests, then the live adapter. Never touches more than one sensor.

privacy-report-analyst

opus · full tools

Owns the NDJSON importer, the schema-versioned decoder, and the identity/domain enrichment layer.

swift-test-runner

haiku · bash + read

Runs the suites, parses failures into structured output. Cheap and called constantly.

docs-security-steward

opus · full tools

Owns docs/ and the security invariants. Details below — this is the one you asked to keep alive.

ui-builder

opus · full tools

SwiftUI shell against replay fixtures only. Kept away from sensor internals on purpose.

Why the researcher agent is not optional

While preparing this report, verification against live Apple sources overturned several things I would otherwise have asserted confidently: that CTCarrier now returns literal "--" placeholders rather than carrier data; that iOS 26 changed local-network privacy (it did not — that was macOS 15); that canOpenURL is fine (deprecated in iOS 27, cap dropping 50→25); that HealthKit needs a paid account (it does not). Apple's platform moves faster than any model's training data. Every ledger row carries a source URL and a verification date because of exactly this.

The TDD loop, per sensor

  1. Research — apple-api-researcher fills the ledger row with sourced facts and the real simulator behaviour.
  2. Red — sensor-implementer writes failing tests against the protocol using the fake. Runs on macOS, in seconds.
  3. Green — minimal implementation. Fake passes.
  4. Adapter — the iOS-only live implementation, kept thin enough to be obviously correct, since it cannot be unit-tested here.
  5. Gate — ledger invariants, plist generation, and the no-egress check all pass.
  6. Document — the steward updates docs/ and classifies the new data.

Sensors are independent, so steps 1–5 fan out cleanly across parallel agents once the first two or three have proven the pattern end to end. A saved workflow in .claude/workflows/ drives that fan-out: one agent per ledger row, pipelined so each sensor moves research→red→green→gate without waiting on its siblings.

Keeping the docs agent alive — the honest answer

There is no true daemon. Claude Code agents are session-scoped, and any claim otherwise is wrong. What exists is three mechanisms that together approximate what you want:

  1. A PostToolUse hook matching Edit|Write appends changed paths to a docs-debt file. Nearly free — it just records.
  2. A Stop hook checks that file at end of turn and dispatches docs-security-steward if there's debt. A Stop hook can return exit code 2 to block the turn ending, so documentation genuinely cannot fall behind within a session.
  3. A scheduled task is the only mechanism that survives session death. A daily run re-verifies stale ledger rows, re-checks the security invariants, and opens issues for drift.

1 and 2 keep docs current during work; 3 catches what happens between sessions. I'd wire all three and treat the schedule as the safety net rather than the primary path.

One note on harness mechanics: hook event names and settings schema have expanded a lot recently, and my own research on the exotic ones came back partly unreliable. The three above are long-standing and safe. Phase 0 verifies the exact schema against your installed version before writing the config, rather than trusting either of us.

The simulator control surface

I checked these against your local Xcode rather than the internet, so the syntax is exact. This is a better testing surface than it's usually given credit for:

  • simctl privacy <dev> grant|revoke|reset <service> <bundle> — deterministic permission states, no dialog. Valid services: calendar, contacts, contacts-limited, location, location-always, photos, photos-add, media-library, microphone, motion, reminders, siri, all.
  • simctl location <dev> start --speed=N --interval=N lat,lon lat,lon … — interpolated GPS tracks. Genuinely excellent: fully repeatable location traces for testing visits, regions, and significant-change logic.
  • simctl addmedia — seeds Photos with your own images (carrying whatever EXIF/GPS you choose) and Contacts from vCards, on top of what's already there.
  • simctl status_bar override — fake battery level and state, cellular bars, operator name, Wi-Fi mode.
  • simctl pbcopy / pbpaste — pasteboard fixtures for the silent-detection work.
  • simctl ui appearance, increase_contrast, content_size for Dynamic Type, simctl push, io recordVideo, keychain. simctl clone plus erase is the right primitive for per-test isolation, and Face ID is genuinely testable once enrolled through the Features menu.
  • Scripting caveat: on a headless boot with Simulator.app not attached, simctl ui and simctl status_bar hang indefinitely. Attach Simulator.app in any automated run, or avoid those two.
Good news I had wrong — the simulator ships with real personal data

I assumed you'd need to seed everything. You don't. A stock iOS 26.2 simulator comes pre-loaded, verified by enumeration:

  • Roughly 90 calendar events across several calendars — birthdays, holidays, found-in-mail — plus a Reminders list. The richest seeded source on the device. (Two probes counted 87 and 92 over different windows, so treat the exact figure as approximate.)
  • 6 contacts with full phone numbers, emails, and postal addresses — the classic Kate Bell / John Appleseed set.
  • 6 photos carrying real GPS coordinates and full EXIF — Iceland, San Francisco, Marin — down to Make=NIKON CORPORATION, Model=NIKON D90, ISO, aperture, and focal length. One has a deliberate -180,-180 sentinel for the no-GPS case, which is a free edge-case fixture.

That changes the plan for the better: the “here is a map of everywhere your camera roll has been” demo — the most visceral thing this app does — is fully buildable today, with no hardware and no fixtures to author. I'd fold Photos-with-EXIF into the Phase 2 vertical slice on the strength of it.

But scripted permissions are less reliable than the help text implies

Measured: simctl privacy grant photos <bundle> writes the TCC record, yet PHPhotoLibrary.authorizationStatus still reported .notDetermined and asset fetches returned zero. The grant has to go through the real prompt from an app with a window. Keep at least one test path that drives the actual dialog rather than trusting the CLI — Apple's own help text warns that simctl privacy bypasses the usage-key requirement and can mask a missing-key bug.

Three gaps that decide the architecture — all measured, not assumed

There is no motion injection. Nothing in simctl feeds accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, or barometer data to a simulator — motion appears in the privacy service list, but only as a permission toggle with no data behind it. A probe run inside a booted iOS 26.2 simulator returned false for every Core Motion availability check, without exception. Core Motion is fake-and-replay-driven by construction, and that is the strongest argument for the three-implementation protocol.

Battery is dead, and the obvious workaround doesn't work. isBatteryMonitoringEnabled refuses to become true, and batteryLevel stays at -1.0. Crucially, status_bar override --batteryLevel does not feed through to UIDevice — it is status-bar chrome only. Proximity monitoring is equally inert, and processorCount/physicalMemory report your Mac rather than a phone.

Several permission states can't be scripted at all — Bluetooth, HealthKit, local network, tracking, speech recognition, notifications, and Focus are absent from simctl privacy, so those flows need a driven UI prompt or a device. Two services do work but aren't in the help text: camera and willow (HomeKit), found by probing and confirmed against the device's TCC database.

The failure mode to design against: silent empty success

The simulator rarely throws when a sensor is missing. It returns something empty, and empty looks like “working code that found nothing.” Each of these was measured:

  • AVCaptureDevice.DiscoverySession returns an empty array, not nil — so devices.first! crashes rather than reporting no camera.
  • CoreTelephony returns an empty dictionary inside a non-nil Optional. Your unwrap succeeds and yields nothing.
  • CBManager.authorization reports .allowedAlways while CBCentralManager.state is .unsupported. Gate on state, never authorization.
  • status_bar battery overrides never reach UIDevice, and simctl privacy grant photos never reaches PhotoKit.

This is a direct argument for the ledger's simulator: column being enforced by tests. A sensor that reports “0 samples, no error” on a simulator and on a broken device looks identical, and only the ledger knows which one you're looking at.

Everything the simulator cannot do accumulates automatically into a device-verification checklist generated from the ledger's simulator: column — so the day you get hardware, the work is already enumerated instead of rediscovered.

06Security

The thing you're building is a very attractive target

You asked for a docs agent with a strong security focus. It's warranted, and not for the usual reasons. This app deliberately concentrates the most sensitive data a phone holds into one local store, and Phase 2's stated goal is to expose it over a public MCP server to third-party AI. That combination is the actual threat, and it deserves to be designed against from the first commit rather than retrofitted.

Phase 1 invariants, enforced by tests

  • No network egress. At all. A test fails the build if URLSession, Network, or any socket API appears outside a narrow allowlist. The one exception is the bundle-ID lookup, which is isolated behind a single named boundary, is the only place allowed to make a request, and is off by default.
  • No analytics, no crash reporting, no third-party SDKs. Zero dependencies is the target; each one added needs a documented reason.
  • Data Protection is explicit per store, defaulting to complete-until-first-unlock, with the choice recorded per sensitivity class rather than inherited by accident.
  • The ledger carries a sensitivity class per sensor, and that class — not developer preference — determines retention, storage protection, and whether a stream is even enabled by default.
  • Nothing is collected without a visible affordance. The app's entire premise is informed exposure; silent collection would be self-defeating even where it's legal.

One thing you're not obliged to do: a privacy manifest (PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy) is an App Store Connect upload gate, not a runtime requirement. The OS doesn't consult it at launch and the simulator doesn't read it, so a sideloaded build needs none. Write one anyway — the required-reason API categories (file timestamps, boot time, disk space, active keyboards, UserDefaults) are precisely what a read-everything app touches, and authoring it now is how capability-tiers.md stays honest about the App Store path.

What the steward owns in docs/

  • threat-model.md — assets, adversaries, and the trust boundary, revisited whenever a sensor lands.
  • data-classification.md — generated from the ledger; every stream classified and justified.
  • capability-tiers.md — the living answer to “what would paid / TestFlight / App Store actually take,” which is the doc you asked for. It stays current because the ledger already tracks free_team and entitlement per row.
  • phase-2-boundary.md — the important one. What must be true before any of this data crosses the device boundary: authn/authz model, scope granularity, redaction and aggregation defaults, revocation, audit logging, and an explicit list of streams that should never be exposed regardless of auth.
  • device-verification.md — generated checklist of everything the simulator cannot prove.
Flagging one thing now, for Phase 2

“Expose useful data over a public MCP server to ChatGPT, Claude, whatever” means handing continuous, real-time access to your location history, health record, contacts, and calendar to third-party model providers under their terms, not yours. That may well be a trade you want to make knowingly — it's your data and your call. It should not be a trade you make by default because Phase 1 happened to leave a port open.

Concretely: Phase 1 ships with no network stack at all, so Phase 2 has to add one deliberately. phase-2-boundary.md gets written during Phase 1, while the constraints are cheap to impose, rather than during Phase 2 when they'd be in the way.

07Sequence

Phases, with exit criteria

Each phase ends on a checkable condition, not a vibe. Phases 2 and 3 are where the agent fleet earns its keep.

P0Harness bootstrapmain session · no fleet

Write .claude/: the six agents, the hooks, permissions allowlisting swift test and xcodebuild. Verify the hook schema against your installed Claude Code version before writing it.

Also upgrade Xcode, and you have no cheaper option. You're on 26.2 with only an iOS 26.2 runtime while shipping iOS is 26.6.1. I tested whether the gap could be closed without a toolchain upgrade: xcodebuild -downloadPlatform iOS -buildVersion was refused for 26.1, 26.3, 26.5, and 26.9 — “not available for download” — while 18.6 began downloading immediately. Apple's index exposes previous majors to an older Xcode, never newer point releases of its own. So developing four point releases behind is structural until you install Xcode 26.6, which is a bad place to stand in a project whose subject is per-version API behaviour.

08Open

What I need from you before Phase 0

Nothing here blocks me from starting, but three of them are cheaper to answer now than to unwind later.

  1. The name. I've used Glasshouse throughout — you live in one, everyone can see in. Say the word and it becomes anything else; it's a rename in one place at this stage and a nuisance later.
  2. A real App Privacy Report export. Turn it on now (Settings → Privacy & Security → App Privacy Report), because it only records from the moment it's enabled and keeps 7 days. Enabling it today means P4 has real data to work with when it arrives. This is the one thing that's genuinely time-sensitive.
  3. Does the device-day budget include $99? It changes what the ledger marks as reachable, and therefore what P3 bothers implementing. My read: start free, decide at P6 with evidence.
  4. How aggressive should the schedule be? The daily steward run costs tokens whether or not there's drift. Daily is my default; weekly is defensible.

My recommendation

Approve P0 through P2 as one unit. The harness only proves itself when a real sensor goes end to end through it, and three sensors is the smallest honest test of that. If the loop works there, P3's fan-out is mechanical; if it doesn't, we'll know before committing a fleet to it.

Measured empirically inside a booted iOS 26.2 simulator

Probe binaries compiled against iPhoneSimulator26.2.sdk and run on a booted iPhone 17 Pro. These are measurements, not inferences:

  • Every Core Motion availability check returns false — accelerometer, gyro, magnetometer, device motion, all six pedometer capabilities, relative and absolute altimeter, motion activity, headphone motion, sensor recorder.
  • HKHealthStore.isHealthDataAvailable() returns true. Health.app and the health daemons ship in the runtime. (Whether requestAuthorization shows a sheet is still untested — it wedged the simulator.)
  • headingAvailable() is false; significantLocationChangeMonitoringAvailable() and circular-region monitoring report available; iBeacon ranging is false.
  • Battery monitoring cannot be enabled; level stays -1.0; status_bar override does not reach UIDevice. Proximity monitoring won't enable either.
  • processorCount and physicalMemory report the host Mac, not a simulated phone.
  • Seeded data enumerated directly: 92 events / 3 calendars, 6 contacts, 6 photos with GPS and full EXIF. Present even on a never-booted device.
  • AVCaptureDevice.DiscoverySession across all 11 device types returns zero video and depth devices; audio returns one non-functional stub with no formats. Microphone capture through AVAudioEngine nonetheless works and captured real host-Mac audio.
  • Vision: RecognizeTextRequest succeeded; face, barcode, saliency, and body-pose requests all failed on the inference context.
  • ARKit and NearbyInteraction report isSupported = false for every configuration. SpeechTranscriber (iOS 26) reports unavailable with zero locales; SFSpeechRecognizer still instantiates.

Corrections to things I would otherwise have asserted

Worth recording, because it's the argument for the researcher agent existing:

  • I would have said HealthKit needs a paid account. It does not.
  • I would have said iOS 26 changed local-network privacy. It didn't — that was macOS 15, and iOS has been unchanged since 14.
  • I would have said canOpenURL was a viable, if weak, detection method. It's deprecated and its cap is halving.
  • I would have assumed LSApplicationWorkspace simply fails. It returns real data in the simulator and empty on device — worse than failing.
  • I would have reached for status_bar override --batteryLevel as a battery fixture. It doesn't reach UIDevice at all.
  • I would have listed CMFallDetectionManager as a sensor. It's API_UNAVAILABLE on iOS — watchOS only, and it won't compile. CMBatchedSensorManager is the nastier variant: it does compile on iOS and simply reports unsupported, so it needs an availability check rather than a compile guard.
  • I would have put CLServiceSession at iOS 17 alongside CLLocationUpdate. It's iOS 18.
  • I would have used NSLocationUsageDescription as a general key. It's macOS-only, and requestAlwaysAuthorization() silently no-ops unless both iOS keys are present.
  • I told you in the first draft that the simulator ships no usable seeded data and that Vision works fully there. Both were wrong: it ships plenty, and Vision is partial. Corrected above.
  • I'd have written NSPhotoLibraryAddOnlyUsageDescription. The real key is NSPhotoLibraryAddUsageDescription.
  • I'd have put CNAuthorizationStatus.limited at iOS 17. It's iOS 18.
